# Reward and Recovery Frameworks v1.0

## Status

Current and authoritative for Book 01 publication development.

This file supersedes earlier reward language that limited Hollow Spire rewards to Discovery Credit and Recovery Cache. Those terms may still be used as internal labels where helpful, but they are no longer the only reward categories and should not be treated as the primary reward structure.

## Core Reward Rule

The Hollow Spire avoids making currency the primary engine of progression.

Rewards should make the world feel deeper, more connected, and more responsive to the party's actions. A reward is successful when it creates at least one of the following outcomes:

- The party learns something meaningful.
- The party gains a new capability.
- The party earns trust, access, or support.
- The party opens a route, settlement service, or future expedition option.
- The party recovers useful materials from the environment or creatures.
- The party leaves a place safer, stranger, clearer, or better than it was found.

Coin, trade goods, and valuables may exist when they make sense, but they should rarely be the most important reward attached to a contract, encounter, discovery, or floor milestone.

## Meaningful Reward Categories

### 1. Discovery Rewards

Discovery rewards represent knowledge, evidence, clues, maps, lore, testimony, or revelations.

Examples:
- A map fragment showing a flooded shortcut.
- A journal naming a lost lantern keeper.
- A seal clue connected to Truth.
- A record explaining why a village was abandoned.
- A route marker that makes future travel safer.

Discovery rewards are especially appropriate when the party investigates, explores, restores records, follows rumors, or solves environmental mysteries.

### 2. Material and Monster-Part Rewards

Material rewards are physical components recovered from creatures, hazards, ruins, fungus beds, flooded passages, broken machinery, or other expedition sites.

Examples:
- Iron Rat Whiskers.
- Cave eel teeth.
- Glowcap spores.
- Lantern moss.
- Deepwater shell fragments.
- Ember beetle carapace plates.
- Broken custodian lenses.

These rewards should feel tied to the ecology of the floor. They may be used for crafting, barter, contract completion, settlement repairs, ritual work, training prerequisites, tool upgrades, or future discoveries.

### 3. Training Rewards

Training rewards represent instruction, mentorship, practice, techniques, or physical adaptation earned through exploration.

Training rewards may include system-facing benefits during internal development, but publication text should keep them generic unless a system-neutral mechanical expression is already defined.

Examples:
- A veteran explorer teaches a safer method for crossing unstable stone.
- A lantern keeper teaches the party how to conserve light during long descents.
- A hunter teaches the party how to track Iron Rat colonies.
- A survivor of Blackwater Run teaches panic control during flooded escapes.
- A settlement mentor unlocks a unique expedition technique.

Possible internal development expressions:
- Bonus proficiency or tool familiarity.
- Additional uses of an existing class feature.
- Improved maximum resilience, stamina, or hit points.
- A special ability unique to the Hollow Spire.
- A situational exploration advantage.
- An extra feat-equivalent advancement, if appropriate for the table.

Publication-facing language should describe the training clearly while avoiding proprietary rules terminology.

### 4. Access Rewards

Access rewards open people, places, services, information, or future choices.

Examples:
- A quest giver agrees to meet the party.
- A settlement grants use of a locked storehouse.
- A ferryman reveals a hidden water route.
- A guild archivist opens restricted records.
- A village grants permission to enter a sealed shrine.

Access rewards are ideal for contracts that build trust or resolve local problems.

### 5. Route and Travel Rewards

Route rewards permanently improve movement through the Spire.

Examples:
- A repaired rope lift.
- A cleared barricade.
- A lantern outpost made safe.
- A flooded passage marked and measured.
- A shortcut between two previously distant locations.
- A safer return path to Emberlight Village or Lantern Village.

These rewards support the campaign's backtracking and exploration identity.

### 6. Settlement and Service Rewards

Settlement rewards improve the lives of NPC communities and create practical benefits for future expeditions.

Examples:
- A healer returns to service.
- A smith can repair expedition gear again.
- A cook begins preparing travel rations.
- Lantern oil production resumes.
- A watch post is restored.
- A frightened witness becomes a reliable contact.

These rewards should visibly show that the party leaves the world better than it was found.

### 7. Equipment and Tool Rewards

Equipment rewards provide useful objects, not necessarily treasure piles.

Examples:
- Lantern upgrades.
- Climbing anchors.
- Waterproof scroll cases.
- Expedition rope.
- Salvaged lenses.
- Surveyor's chalk.
- A repaired outpost key.
- A strange relic that hints at a later floor.

Equipment rewards should support exploration, problem solving, survival, or future route access.

### 8. Reputation and Relationship Rewards

Relationship rewards change how people treat the party.

Examples:
- Emberlight villagers begin sharing rumors.
- Guild scouts trust the party with harder contracts.
- A rescued NPC becomes a recurring ally.
- A suspicious settlement allows the party to sleep safely.
- A rival explorer offers information instead of hostility.

These rewards are especially useful when a contract's value is social rather than material.

### 9. Narrative Progression Rewards

Narrative rewards move the campaign forward.

Examples:
- A lead toward Blackwater Run.
- A clue about Lantern Village.
- Evidence of the Moon Lantern's location.
- A revelation tied to the First Seal of Truth.
- A Warden Ledger reflection opportunity.

Narrative rewards should never feel like mere exposition. They should create choices, questions, or new routes.

## Deprecated Reward Concepts

### Guild Marks

Guild Marks are removed from canon.

Any existing reference to Guild Marks should be flagged during audit and replaced with a meaningful reward category such as access, training, materials, reputation, route improvement, equipment, or narrative progression.

### Currency-First Contracts

Contracts should not be built primarily around payment unless the story specifically requires it.

When an older contract offers only money, replace or supplement the reward with a meaningful non-currency benefit.

## Discovery Credit and Recovery Cache

The older terms Discovery Credit and Recovery Cache are not forbidden, but they are now optional internal tools.

Use them only when they help organize manuscript development.

They should not limit reward design and should not appear as the only reward types in publication-facing Chapter 0 material.

## Reward Audit Questions

When auditing any encounter, contract, location, or floor milestone, ask:

1. Does this reward rely too heavily on currency?
2. Does this reward create a new choice, capability, contact, route, or piece of knowledge?
3. Could a monster-part or environmental material make this feel more grounded?
4. Could a local NPC offer training instead of payment?
5. Could the reward improve a settlement, outpost, or travel route?
6. Does the reward reinforce exploration, restoration, survival, or wonder?
7. Does the reward avoid Guild Marks?
8. Does the reward avoid proprietary rules language in publication-facing text?

If the answer to most of these questions is no, revise the reward.
